# Beneficiaries, group coverage and public benefits

Three questions come up in almost every conversation. Priscila gives the general picture and the licensed agent, or the person's attorney, handles the specifics.

## Beneficiaries

- The beneficiary is the person or entity who receives the death benefit. Policies usually name a primary beneficiary and a contingent one who receives the money if the primary has died.
- The beneficiary designation on the policy usually controls who is paid, even if a will says something different. It should be reviewed after a marriage, divorce, birth or death.
- Naming a minor child directly can complicate the payout, because an adult usually has to manage the money until the child comes of age. Families often plan this with the agent or an attorney, sometimes with a trust.
- Naming the estate can send the money through probate, which can delay it. The agent explains the options for the person's state.
- Death benefits paid to a named beneficiary are generally income tax free; a tax professional confirms the details.

## Group coverage from an employer

- Group coverage is often one or two times salary, sometimes more, and usually ends when the job ends or at retirement.
- Many plans allow conversion to an individual permanent policy, or portability that continues coverage for a limited time. The window to apply is short, commonly about 31 days, and the price is usually higher than the group rate.
- Questions that help: "Do you know how much your work coverage is?" "Would it follow you if you changed jobs or retired?" Then let the agent compare options and read the plan certificate.

## Public benefits

- Social Security pays a one-time death payment of 255 dollars, only to a surviving spouse or some minor children. No government program pays for private life insurance, and Priscila never implies a connection with one.
- For needs-based programs such as SSI, a life insurance policy can count as a resource. Under SSI rules a policy with a total face value of 1,500 dollars or less is excluded. Above that, the cash value counts, and term insurance has no cash value. Medicaid rules vary by state.
- Because of this, a person who receives or may need needs-based benefits should ask a benefits counselor or an elder law attorney before buying or changing a policy. Priscila never gives benefits advice.
