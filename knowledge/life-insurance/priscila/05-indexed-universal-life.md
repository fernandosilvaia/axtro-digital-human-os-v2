# Indexed universal life (IUL): honest education

IUL is permanent life insurance with flexible premiums and a cash value whose interest credit is linked to the performance of a market index, such as the S&P 500, without the money being invested in the market directly. It combines a death benefit with potential cash value growth, and it requires disciplined, consistent funding.

## How the crediting works

- Each crediting period the policy looks at the index change and credits interest based on that change, limited by a cap rate (the maximum credited), a participation rate (the share of the index gain used) and sometimes a spread (a flat percentage subtracted).
- Example to explain the logic: with a 7 percent cap, a 12 percent index gain credits 7 percent. With a 75 percent participation rate, a 10 percent gain credits 7.5 percent.
- A floor, often 0 percent, means index losses are not credited as negative interest. This does not mean the cash value cannot go down: policy charges (cost of insurance, administrative and rider charges) are deducted every month regardless of the index, so in years with low or zero credits the cash value can fall and coverage can end if the account runs out.
- Caps, participation rates and spreads are usually not guaranteed. The carrier can change them going forward within contract limits.
- Dividends paid by the companies in the index are typically not part of the index change used for crediting.

## Illustrations and regulation

Sales illustrations project future values using assumed rates, usually the same rate every year, while real index results vary from year to year. NAIC Actuarial Guideline 49-A limits the rates and leverage that an IUL illustration can show, because aggressive illustrations misled consumers in the past. A revision adopted in late 2025 applies to illustrations on policies sold on or after April 1, 2026. Among other changes, an index with less than 10 years of history cannot be shown with a historical table, and the illustration must say that historical results are not indicative of future returns. These rules limit what an illustration may show. They do not change what the policy actually credits. The licensed agent must show both the guaranteed and the non-guaranteed columns.

## Taxes, loans and the MEC line

- The death benefit is generally income tax free to beneficiaries; a tax professional confirms the details.
- Cash value can be accessed through withdrawals and policy loans. Whether they are tax free depends on the policy not being a modified endowment contract (MEC) and staying in force until death; a tax professional confirms.
- Overfunding beyond federal limits turns the policy into a MEC, which changes how loans and withdrawals are taxed.
- Loans accrue interest. Some loan types keep earning an index credit while charging interest, so the net cost can swing. If loans plus charges exceed the cash value, the policy can lapse and the gain can become taxable. Lapse risk is the most important risk to explain.
- Tax rules are complex: the person should confirm with a tax professional.

## Who it may fit

People with a permanent coverage need, stable income, a long time horizon, who can fund the policy consistently and understand the moving parts. Some people use the cash value for later needs; the agent explains how that works and the risks, and it never replaces emergency savings.

## Lines that never move

- Never promise that the cash value can only go up, never describe it as market gains without risk, and never promise tax free retirement income.
- A 0 percent floor does not mean "I can't lose": charges still apply.
- Never compare it to a 401(k) or a brokerage account as if it were the same kind of product.
- Never steer toward IUL because it pays higher commission. The licensed agent decides after seeing the full picture.
